1. Grid Emissions Intensity Trends by State

Scope 2 Emission Factors by State (kg CO2-e / kWh) NGA Factors

Source: DCCEEW National Greenhouse Accounts Factors, editions 2014–2025. FY2023 and FY2024 values confirmed; earlier years interpolated.

Key Observations

  • South Australia has seen the steepest decline in emissions intensity, falling from ~0.55 to 0.23 kg CO2-e/kWh over the decade — a ~58% reduction driven by massive wind and solar deployment (74% renewable generation in 2024).[1]
  • Victoria remains the highest-intensity NEM state due to continued brown coal reliance (Latrobe Valley), though it has fallen from ~1.13 to 0.77 — a ~32% reduction as wind has grown to ~20% of generation.[2]
  • Tasmania has consistently had the lowest intensity (~0.12–0.19 kg CO2-e/kWh), reflecting its predominantly hydroelectric generation (71% hydro, 19% wind in 2024).[1]
  • NSW and QLD have seen moderate declines driven by coal plant retirements and growing solar. NSW coal share fell from ~82% to ~55% over the period; QLD from ~72% to ~50%.[3]
  • WA operates a separate grid (SWIS) and has a gas-dominated generation profile (59% gas in 2024). Its decline has been more gradual.[1]
  • NT is heavily gas-dependent (83% gas in 2024) with minimal renewables penetration.[1]

2. National Emissions Intensity Trend

Australia — National Average Grid Emissions Intensity Multiple sources

Sources: DCCEEW NGA Factors (national average); Climate Change Authority; Australian Energy Council; Low Carbon Power. Values are approximate annual averages.

The national emissions intensity of electricity generation has declined from approximately 0.88 kg CO2-e/kWh in 2008 to ~0.47 kg CO2-e/kWh in 2024 — a reduction of nearly 47%.[4][5] Key drivers include:

  • Closure of ageing coal-fired power stations (notably Hazelwood in VIC, 2017; Liddell in NSW, 2023)
  • Rapid growth in wind and solar generation (renewables reached 36% of total generation in 2024, a record high)[1]
  • A temporary dip during the carbon pricing period (2012–2014), with a 4.5% intensity reduction in 2012–13[6]
  • Total grid-connected scope 1 emissions declined from ~167 Mt CO2-e (FY2017–18) to 133.7 Mt CO2-e (FY2024–25)[7]

Storage Capital Cost by Technology & Duration (AUD/kWh, 2024–25) CSIRO GenCost

Source: CSIRO GenCost 2024–25, Appendix Tables B.4–B.8. Total installed cost basis (includes balance of system). Battery costs are for utility-scale lithium-ion. Pumped hydro costs are site-dependent. All values in real 2024–25 AUD.[10]

Key Observations — Generation & Storage Costs

  • Solar PV is now the cheapest source of new electricity generation in Australia at A$43–73/MWh, followed by onshore wind at A$70–116/MWh. Both are cheaper than any new-build fossil fuel or nuclear option.[10]
  • New coal is uncompetitive: New black coal (A$102–164/MWh) and brown coal (A$131–211/MWh) significantly exceed renewable alternatives even before carbon costs are factored in.
  • Nuclear remains the most expensive option: Large-scale nuclear at A$155–252/MWh and SMR at A$400–663/MWh reflect high capital costs (A$8,655/kW and A$29,667/kW respectively) and long construction times.[10]
  • Battery storage costs continue to fall rapidly. Utility-scale 4-hour lithium-ion batteries are at A$423/kWh capital cost, down from ~A$800/kWh in 2020. GenCost projects a further 31% decline to A$290/kWh by 2040.[10]
  • Pumped hydro is cost-effective for long duration — at A$163/kWh for 48-hour systems, it undercuts batteries for multi-day storage, though site selection constraints limit deployment.

9. Gas vs Heat Pump Heating — Emissions & Cost Breakeven by State

Comparing the emissions intensity and running cost of delivering one GJ of useful space heating via natural gas (ducted heater) versus an electric reverse-cycle heat pump, for each Australian state. The breakeven depends on each state’s grid carbon intensity, local energy prices, and climate-dependent heat pump efficiency.

Emissions per GJ Useful Heat (kg CO2-e) NGA Factors

Gas heating assumes 85% efficient ducted heater with NGA scope 1 factor of 55.5 kg CO2-e/GJ (incl. CO2, CH4, N2O). Dashed line shows full fuel-cycle gas emissions including upstream scope 3 (~68.5 kg/GJ). Heat pump COP varies by state climate zone (2.8–5.0). Grid emission factors are FY2023–24 from NGA Factors.[2][13]

Running Cost per GJ Useful Heat (AUD) AEMC / AER 2024–25

Residential energy prices from AEMC price trends and AER default market offers (2024–25). Gas not available in TAS or NT (no reticulated network). Heat pump cost = electricity price × 277.8 kWh/GJ ÷ COP. Gas cost = gas price ÷ heater efficiency.[13][14]

Key Observations — Heating Breakeven

  • Heat pumps produce lower emissions than gas heaters in 6 of 7 states (all except Victoria on scope 1 gas only). When upstream gas supply-chain emissions are included (scope 3), heat pumps win in every state including Victoria.
  • South Australia and Tasmania show the most dramatic advantage — heat pump emissions are 70–80% lower than gas, reflecting their very low grid carbon intensity (0.23 and 0.15 kg CO2-e/kWh respectively).
  • Victoria is the closest to breakeven due to its brown coal-heavy grid (0.77 kg CO2-e/kWh) combined with colder winters reducing heat pump COP to ~3.2. However, Victoria’s grid is decarbonising rapidly (−34% since FY2014), and the breakeven tips further toward heat pumps each year.
  • On cost, heat pumps are cheaper to run in NSW, QLD, SA, and VIC where gas prices range from $33–60/GJ. Queensland ($60/GJ) and SA ($55/GJ) show the largest cost advantage for heat pumps. In WA, lower gas prices (~$25/GJ) narrow the gap but heat pumps remain competitive.
  • TAS and NT have no reticulated gas networks, so electric heating is the only piped option. Tasmania’s near-zero-carbon grid makes heat pumps exceptionally clean. The NT has minimal heating demand (tropical climate).
  • These figures exclude capital costs. A new reverse-cycle heat pump costs ~A$2,000–5,000 installed; a ducted gas heater ~A$3,000–6,000. Several states offer rebates for heat pump installation (e.g. VIC Energy Upgrades, NSW Energy Savings Scheme, SA Retailer Energy Productivity Scheme).

11. Assumptions & Methodology

  • Greenhouse gas emission scopes are defined by the GHG Protocol and used in the NGER Act:
    • Scope 1 — Direct emissions from sources owned or controlled by the reporting entity (e.g. combustion of fuel in a power station's boiler, fugitive methane from coal mines). Reported by generators.
    • Scope 2 — Indirect emissions from the generation of purchased electricity, heating, or cooling consumed by the reporting entity. This is what an electricity consumer “inherits” from the grid. This report focuses on scope 2 location-based factors.
    • Scope 3 — All other indirect emissions across the value chain — upstream (fuel extraction, transport, infrastructure manufacture) and downstream (transmission losses, end-of-life). Scope 3 is not included in NGA Factors for electricity.
  • Scope 2 (location-based) factors are used throughout. These represent the average emissions intensity of electricity generated within each state/territory grid, adjusted for interstate electricity transfers where applicable (NEM states). They are the standard metric for organisational carbon accounting in Australia. A “market-based” scope 2 approach (using contractual instruments like GreenPower or LGCs) would yield different values but is not covered here.
  • Financial year basis: All emission factor values are on an Australian financial year basis (July–June). "FY2024" refers to 1 July 2023 – 30 June 2024. Generation mix data uses calendar year 2024.
  • NEM regions map approximately to states: NSW1 includes ACT; VIC1 = Victoria; QLD1 = Queensland; SA1 = South Australia; TAS1 = Tasmania. WA operates a separate grid (SWIS/WEM). NT operates the DKIS grid.
  • Confirmed data points: NGA Factors 2024 edition (for FY2023–24 reporting) and NGA Factors 2023 edition (for FY2022–23 reporting) were directly verified from published values. An earlier educational reference (~FY2013–14) provided anchor values: VIC 1.17, NSW 0.87, QLD 0.82, WA 0.78, SA 0.62, TAS 0.20, NT 0.69.
  • Interpolation method: For years between confirmed data points, values were linearly interpolated and cross-checked against: (a) the known national average trend, (b) published NEM-wide intensity data from AEMO CDEII, and (c) the trajectory of renewable generation share in each state. Interpolated values are indicative only.
  • Methodology change (2023): In August 2023, DCCEEW discontinued the three-year rolling average for calculating scope 2 factors. Factors now use single-year renewable generation data from AEMO NEM-Review, aligned with the latest NGER generation data. This may cause small discontinuities in the trend.[9]
  • Fuel source breakdown is estimated by combining each fuel type's approximate share of state generation (from AEMO and OpenNEM data) with the fuel type's typical emission factor (from AEMO generator registrations and NGA Factors). The per-fuel contribution is: fuel_share × fuel_emission_factor.
  • Renewables (wind, solar PV, hydro) are assigned zero direct (scope 1) emissions, consistent with NGA methodology. Lifecycle emissions are excluded.
  • Brown coal applies to Victoria only (Latrobe Valley generators). All other coal generation in Australia is black coal.
  • Gas includes both combined-cycle (CCGT, ~0.4–0.5 tCO2-e/MWh) and open-cycle gas turbines (OCGT, ~0.6–0.7 tCO2-e/MWh). A blended gas factor is used per state based on estimated CCGT/OCGT mix.
  • WA and NT data is less granular than NEM states due to separate grid operators. WA factors cover the SWIS (South West Interconnected System) only; isolated grids (e.g. NWIS at ~0.61 kg CO2-e/kWh) are excluded. NT covers the DKIS (Darwin–Katherine Interconnected System).
  • Rooftop solar: Self-consumption of small-scale rooftop solar is deducted from grid generation in NGA factor calculations (since 2023 methodology update).[9]
